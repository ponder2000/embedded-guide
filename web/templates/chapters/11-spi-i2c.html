SECTION 01

When the chips share a clock

UART works without a shared clock — both sides just agree on a baud rate ahead of time and hope their oscillators stay close. That's fine for talking to a PC, but on a crowded circuit board, where your MCU needs to chat with a sensor, a display, and a flash chip an inch away, there's a faster, simpler idea: send the clock alongside the data.

A synchronous bus carries an explicit clock line. Every bit is sampled on a clock edge, so there's no guessing about timing and no drift to tolerate. The two workhorse synchronous buses in embedded systems are SPI and I²C — both let one controller talk to many chips, but they make opposite trade-offs.

The core idea: shipping the clock with the data removes the one thing async links fight hardest — timing tolerance. That's why board-level peripherals almost always speak a synchronous bus.
SECTION 02

SPI — four wires, full speed

SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) is the simplest fast bus there is. The controller drives a clock and two data lines; a third pick line selects which chip is listening. Data flows both directions on every clock edge — controller and peripheral are just two shift registers stitched into a loop.

  • SCLK — the clock, always driven by the controller.
  • MOSI — Controller Out, Peripheral In (data leaving the controller).
  • MISO — Controller In, Peripheral Out (data coming back).
  • CS / SS — Chip Select, one dedicated line per peripheral, active-low.
Full-duplex is SPI's quiet superpower: as the controller shifts a byte out on MOSI, a byte from the peripheral shifts in on MISO at the same time. There's no addressing and no acknowledgement — selecting a chip is the addressing, and that's why you need one CS pin per peripheral.
SECTION 03

I²C — two wires, many chips

I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit) trades raw speed for pin economy. The entire bus is just two open-drain wires — SDA (data) and SCL (clock) — held high by pull-up resistors. Any device can pull a line low; nobody ever drives it high. That single trick lets dozens of chips share the same two wires without ever shorting each other.

Because everyone shares the wires, I²C addresses each chip by a 7-bit number baked into it. A transfer is a tidy little frame: a START condition, the address plus a read/write bit, an acknowledgement, then data bytes — each one acknowledged — and finally a STOP.

Now compare the two side by side. Flip between them and watch what each protocol asks of your board.

SPI — 4 wires: SCLK · MOSI · MISO · CS

  • Wiring: shared SCLK, MOSI, MISO; plus one dedicated CS line per peripheral.
  • Direction: full-duplex — sends and receives on every clock edge at once.
  • Speed: fast, tens of MHz and up; no protocol overhead per byte.
  • Selection: no addresses — pulling a chip's CS low is the addressing.
  • Cost: pin count grows with every device you add.

I²C — 2 wires: SDA · SCL

  • Wiring: just two open-drain lines with pull-up resistors — no matter how many chips.
  • Direction: half-duplex — one direction at a time, sharing the SDA line.
  • Speed: slower (100 kHz–1 MHz typical); start/address/ACK overhead per transfer.
  • Selection: each chip has a 7-bit address; ACK/NACK confirms it heard you.
  • Cost: add devices for free on the pin budget — limited only by addresses and capacitance.
Try this: toggle to I²C and notice the wire count never changes no matter how many chips hang off the bus — then toggle back to SPI and remember each new chip costs another GPIO pin. That single difference drives most real-world choices.
SECTION 04

So which one do you reach for?

There's no universal winner — only the right tool for the wiring in front of you. The decision almost always comes down to three numbers: how fast you need to move data, how many pins you can spare, and how many devices must share the bus.

Reach for SPI when

You need throughput — driving a display, streaming from an SD card or fast ADC, or talking to external flash. You have GPIO pins to spare for chip-selects, and only a handful of peripherals. Full-duplex and zero per-byte overhead make it the speed king.

Reach for I²C when

You're collecting from many slow chips — temperature, humidity, an RTC, a small EEPROM, port expanders. Pins are precious. Two wires carry the whole bus, addressing keeps everyone straight, and the modest speed is plenty for sensor reads.

In practice a single board often runs both: I²C as a low-pin "housekeeping" bus for sensors and configuration, and SPI as a dedicated high-speed lane to the one or two chips that need bandwidth.
The rule of thumb: SPI buys speed and full-duplex with pins; I²C buys devices and pin economy with speed. Pick the trade you can afford — and don't be surprised when the answer is "both."
