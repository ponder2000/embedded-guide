SECTION 01

Three ideas before the first wire

Software can request a high pin, but electricity decides what happens next. Start with voltage, current, and resistance: enough intuition to build a small LED circuit without treating the board like a magic power source.

Voltage (V) is a difference in electrical potential between two points; always ask “relative to what?” Current (I) is the rate of charge flow, measured in amperes (A), often milliamperes (mA). Resistance (R), measured in ohms (Ω), opposes that flow. Water pressure, flow, and a narrow pipe are useful analogies, not literal descriptions.

For a resistor, V = I × R, so I = V / R. An LED is not a resistor: it needs a series resistor to limit current. Assume a red LED drops about 2.0 V. From a 3.3 V supply, the resistor gets 3.3 − 2.0 = 1.3 V. With 330 Ω, current is 1.3 / 330 = 0.00394 A = 3.94 mA.

The drop varies with LED type, temperature, and current; this is an estimate, not a guaranteed operating point. A real GPIO high can also fall below its supply voltage under load.

SECTION 03

GPIO is a signal, not a power socket

A GPIO can source or sink only limited current. Read your exact MCU datasheet and board schematic: check recommended operating conditions, output-voltage guarantees at load, per-pin current, and combined port/chip limits. Absolute maximum ratings are damage boundaries, not design targets. Several individually modest LEDs can still exceed a shared limit.

Ground is the voltage reference and return path. Low-voltage boards exchanging non-isolated signals normally need a common ground. Connect their grounds, not their different supply rails. A 5 V signal can damage a 3.3 V input: use suitable level translation unless the exact pin and operating conditions explicitly allow it. A board accepting 5 V through USB does not make its GPIO 5 V tolerant.

Keep this lesson low-voltage: no mains wiring. Never connect a motor directly to GPIO; use a rated driver and appropriate inductive-load protection. Do not connect unknown powered equipment or join power sources casually.
SECTION 04

From schematic to breadboard

On a typical solderless breadboard, each five-hole strip connects internally; the center gap separates the two sides. Power rails run lengthwise but may be split halfway. Confirm your board's connections with power disconnected; printed lines alone are not proof.

An LED's anode usually has the longer lead; the cathode usually has a shorter lead and flat case edge. Trimmed leads and unusual packages defeat those clues: verify its datasheet. Both legs in one connected strip bypass the LED. Check the board pinout too: a header position, a GPIO name, and a chip package pin number are different things.

  • Power off: identify GND, the intended GPIO, LED polarity, resistor value, and rail breaks.
  • Trace the loop: GPIO → resistor → anode → cathode → GND. The resistor may go on either side, but must remain in series.
  • Check ratings: confirm voltage compatibility, LED current, resistor power, and GPIO per-pin/total budgets.
  • Try safely: after verification, power up and toggle slowly. If it stays dark, disconnect power before changing wires; never “fix” it by removing the resistor.
