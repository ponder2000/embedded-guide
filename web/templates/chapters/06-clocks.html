SECTION 01

Everything marches to a steady beat

A digital chip is fundamentally synchronous: flip-flops latch new values only on a clock edge. The CPU fetches an instruction, a timer counts up, a UART shifts out a bit — each one happens on a tick. Take the clock away and the silicon simply freezes mid-thought.

That heartbeat comes from an oscillator. You usually get a choice of source, and the trade-off is always the same: convenience versus precision.

  • Internal RC oscillator (the HSI, ~8 MHz on an STM32F1): already on the die, free, instant. But it drifts with temperature and voltage — often ±1% or worse. Fine for blinking an LED, fatal for timing a serial link.
  • External crystal (the HSE, an 8 MHz quartz can soldered beside the chip): costs a part and two pins, takes a few milliseconds to stabilise. In exchange you get accuracy measured in parts-per-million.
Frequency and period are two views of the same thing: f = 1 / T. An 8 MHz crystal swings high-low-high every 125 ns. Halve the frequency and the period doubles — that simple inverse is exactly what a prescaler exploits.
SECTION 02

From one crystal to a whole tree

An 8 MHz crystal is slow for a CPU and wrong for half your peripherals. So the chip takes that one source, multiplies it up through a PLL to make SYSCLK, then divides it back down with prescalers to feed each bus at the speed it can tolerate.

Walk it left to right: the 8 MHz HSE feeds a PLL that multiplies by N, producing SYSCLK. The AHB prescaler divides SYSCLK into HCLK (the core and DMA bus). The APB prescaler then divides HCLK again into PCLK, which clocks the slower peripherals. Set the dials below and watch the numbers fall out.

SECTION 03

A faster clock is not a free lunch

Cranking SYSCLK higher means more instructions per second — but every edge also charges and discharges the chip's internal capacitance, and dynamic power scales roughly with frequency. Speed costs current, and battery-powered designs feel it directly.

The deeper trap is that peripherals derive their timing from the bus clock you handed them. A UART computes its baud divisor from PCLK; an SPI shifts at a fraction of PCLK; a timer counts at HCLK. Change a prescaler and forget to recompute, and the symptoms are maddening:

  • Garbled serial bytes — the baud rate drifted because PCLK changed under the divisor.
  • A "1 second" delay loop that now fires twice as fast or twice as slow.
  • A sensor read at the wrong SPI rate returning plausible-but-wrong values.
This is why clock setup lives in the very first lines of main(), before any peripheral is touched. Pick SYSCLK, set the prescalers, then configure the UART against a clock you know won't move.
The mental model: the clock tree is a contract. Every peripheral driver was written assuming a specific input frequency. Choose your clocks deliberately, write them down, and let the rest of the firmware compute its timing from those known numbers — never from a guess.
