SECTION 01

Two wires, no clock

UART is the friendliest way two chips talk: a single wire each direction, and no shared clock line. The catch — without a clock to mark each bit, both sides must agree in advance how fast bits arrive. That agreed speed is the baud rate.

When nothing is being sent, the line sits high (idle). To announce a byte, the transmitter drops the line low for one bit period — the start bit. That falling edge is the receiver's only cue: it says "a frame begins now, start your clock." Eight data bits follow, then the line returns high for a stop bit, ready for the next byte.

UART stands for Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter. "Asynchronous" is the whole point: there is no clock wire — timing is reconstructed from the start edge plus a baud rate both ends were told ahead of time.
SECTION 02

Framing & baud, then build a byte

Each bit occupies one bit period = 1 / baud seconds. At 115200 baud, that's about 8.68 µs per bit. The receiver doesn't sample at a bit's edge — it waits half a period after the start edge, then samples in the middle of every bit, where the voltage is most settled.

This is why both sides must use the same baud. If the receiver's bit period is even a few percent off, its sample points drift away from the bit centres; by the eighth bit it may be reading the neighbour's value, and you get garbage on screen.

SECTION 03

Crossed wires & the printf you can see

Wiring UART is almost trivially simple — three connections — but there's one rule everyone forgets exactly once: TX connects to RX, not TX to TX. Each device's transmit line must reach the other's receive line, so the cabling crosses over.

Pick a baud rate from a small, conventional menu. 9600 is the slow, rock-solid default for sensors and legacy gear; 115200 is the modern workhorse for fast logs. Both ends just have to match. Other common rates — 19200, 38400, 57600 — fill the gap, but you rarely need them.

9600 baud

≈ 104 µs per bit · ~960 bytes/sec. Forgiving of clock error and long, noisy wires. The safe choice when you're not sure.

115200 baud

≈ 8.68 µs per bit · ~11.5 KB/sec. The everyday rate for debug consoles — fast enough that printf logs don't slow your loop.

Why you'll love it: UART is the embedded developer's printf channel. Wire TX to a USB-serial adapter, open a terminal at the matching baud, and your board can talk — printing variables, errors, and boot messages. When a chip seems dead, UART is usually the first window you open into what it's actually doing.
