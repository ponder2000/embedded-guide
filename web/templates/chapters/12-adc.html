SECTION 01

The world is analog, the chip is digital

A temperature, a microphone, a light sensor, a battery — out in the real world these are smooth, continuous voltages that can sit at any value at all. Your MCU only understands integers. The Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) is the bridge: it measures a voltage and hands the CPU a whole number.

An ADC works against a fixed reference voltage, Vref. It maps any input voltage in the range [0, Vref] onto an integer code in [0, 2<sup>N</sup> − 1], where N is the converter's bit count. Feed it 0 V and you read 0; feed it Vref and you read the maximum code.

The ADC throws away two things at once: it samples the smooth signal only at discrete instants (sampling, Section 3), and at each instant it rounds the voltage to the nearest of a finite set of levels (quantisation, Section 2). Both losses are tunable trade-offs, not accidents.
SECTION 02

Resolution & the quantisation step

An N-bit ADC divides the [0, Vref] range into 2<sup>N</sup> equal levels. The width of one level — the smallest voltage difference the ADC can resolve — is the LSB (least significant bit): LSB = Vref / 2<sup>N</sup>. Any input gets rounded down to the nearest level boundary, and the leftover is the quantisation error.

Each extra bit halves the LSB and doubles the number of levels — so the staircase tracks the ramp more tightly and the worst-case error shrinks. Try it: drop the resolution to 1 bit and the ADC can only tell you "low or high"; crank it to 8 bits and the steps almost vanish.

SECTION 03

Sample fast enough, or alias

Resolution fixes the vertical axis; the horizontal axis is governed by sampling rate. The Nyquist–Shannon theorem says you must sample at more than twice the highest frequency present in the signal. Sample too slowly and a fast wave masquerades as a slow one — an alias — and no amount of resolution can undo it.

Two defences. First, a sample-and-hold circuit freezes the input on a tiny capacitor the instant conversion begins, so the voltage can't drift while the converter does its work. Second, an analog anti-aliasing low-pass filter strips out frequencies above half the sampling rate before they can fold down into your band of interest.

In practice you'll meet this on real silicon constantly: an STM32 typically carries a 12-bit successive-approximation ADC — 4096 levels, an LSB of about 0.8 mV at Vref = 3.3 V — with a configurable sample time and multiplexed channels feeding a single converter.

The big idea: an ADC reading is only as good as its two halves. Pick enough bits so the LSB is smaller than the detail you care about, and a sampling rate above twice your signal's fastest wiggle. Get either wrong and the digital number is a confident, precise lie.
