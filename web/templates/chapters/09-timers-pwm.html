SECTION 01

A timer is a counter that never tires

Strip away the marketing and a hardware timer is one register that increments on every tick of a clock. Feed it a known rate and it becomes a ruler for time itself — counting up, wrapping around, and nudging your code at exact intervals while the CPU does other work.

On an STM32 the peripheral is called TIMx. Three pieces do the work:

  • The prescaler (PSC) divides the incoming clock — the same clock tree you met in the clocks chapter. A 72 MHz timer clock with PSC = 71 ticks the counter at 1 MHz.
  • The counter (CNT) climbs one step per tick: 0, 1, 2, …
  • The auto-reload (ARR) is the ceiling. When CNT reaches ARR it wraps back to 0 — an update event — and can fire an interrupt.
The period in plain arithmetic: with a timer clock ftim, one wrap takes (PSC + 1) × (ARR + 1) / ftim seconds. Pick PSC for a convenient tick, then ARR for the period you want.
SECTION 02

PWM is the counter racing a compare value

Put a second register beside the counter — the capture/compare register CCR — and wire a pin to a simple rule: drive it high while CNT < CCR, low otherwise. As the counter sweeps from 0 to ARR each period, the pin spends a fixed fraction of every cycle high. That fraction is the duty cycle.

SECTION 03

Why a square wave can look like a dimmer knob

The pin only ever outputs 0V or 3.3V — it never sits at half voltage. Yet by flicking between them thousands of times a second, the average voltage lands anywhere you like. Slow things average it for you, and PWM quietly becomes an analog control with none of the analog cost.

Servos are the odd one out: they care about pulse width, not duty ratio. A 50 Hz frame (20 ms) with a 1 ms high pulse means full left, 1.5 ms centre, 2 ms full right — same timer, you just hold ARR fixed and vary CCR for the narrow pulse.
The whole chapter in one line: a timer counts time, ARR sets the period, CCR sets the duty, and duty × supply is the average voltage you hand to the world — dimming an LED, spinning a motor, or steering a servo from a single pin that only knows on and off.
